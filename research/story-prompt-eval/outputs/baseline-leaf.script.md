# A leaf trades water for carbon

## Beat 1 — One opening, two consequences

Content needed: One schematic broad leaf and a selected patch of its lower surface. Introduce a single open stoma as a gap between two guard cells, continuous with air spaces inside the leaf. Preserve this patch and pore for the following scenes. Do not show gas-flow answers yet.

Narration: A leaf needs carbon dioxide from the air to make sugar. But letting that gas in can let water out. How can the same tiny opening do both?

## Beat 2 — A shared passage

Content needed: Continue into the selected pore and a schematic cutaway of the moist interior. Identify stomata, then show carbon dioxide entering and being used in photosynthesis, followed by water evaporating and water vapor escaping through the same opening. Use irregular gas motion in both directions; any arrows represent overall movement, not one-way permission. Particle counts and sizes are illustrative.

Narration: These openings are called stomata. An open stoma connects outside air with spaces inside the leaf. There, cells use light energy to make sugars from carbon dioxide and water. As photosynthesis uses carbon dioxide, more can move in from the outside air.

Narration: Meanwhile, water evaporates from moist surfaces inside the leaf. When the outside air is drier, more water vapor moves out than comes back in. Gas molecules move in both directions through the opening; these arrows show the overall movement. So the pore does not separate useful carbon dioxide from escaping water. The same open passage allows both exchanges. That is why gaining carbon can cost the leaf water.

## Beat 3 — Narrowing the passage

Content needed: Keep the same pore, guard cells, moist interior, and gas identities. Make the outside air drier, showing fewer water-vapor particles outside and a greater tendency for outward loss while the pore is still open. Then show the guard cells changing shape and narrowing the pore in response to dry-condition signals. Show both exchanges becoming more restricted, and retain a small amount of water loss through the leaf surface. End on the same leaf and selected patch with the tradeoff visible, without numerical rates.

Narration: Now imagine the air becomes much drier. With less water vapor outside, water escapes more readily through an open pore. If the leaf loses water faster than its roots replace it, it can dry out.

Narration: In dry conditions, signals can make the two guard cells around a pore lose water and change shape, narrowing the opening. This is a physical response, with no thinking or planning involved.

Narration: A narrower passage slows water loss, but also limits fresh carbon dioxide entering, so photosynthesis can slow. Closing stomata does not instantly stop every water loss: some water still escapes through the leaf's outer surface. Opening gives carbon dioxide easier access at a water cost; closing helps conserve water while restricting that access.
