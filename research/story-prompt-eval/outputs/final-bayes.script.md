# Why a positive screen is not a diagnosis

## Beat 1 — Two ways to test positive

Content needed: A hypothetical cohort of 1,000 contains 10 people with the condition and 990 without it. Blue means condition present; grey means condition absent. A positive result is a white outline, not a change in condition color. Nine of the ten with the condition test positive, a sensitivity of 90%. The false-positive rate is 10% among those without the condition. Before the pause, show the group sizes and rates but hide the count of false positives, the combined positive total, and the posterior probability. Reveal 99 false positives only after the pause. This is a toy probability model, not medical guidance.

Narration: Imagine a screening test in a hypothetical population of one thousand people. Ten have a condition, and nine hundred ninety do not. This is a toy probability example, not medical guidance.

Narration: The test catches nine of the ten people with the condition. That ninety percent is called its sensitivity. But it also gives a positive result to ten percent of people without the condition. Those results are false positives. A positive result can therefore come from either group.

Invitation (spoken): Which group will contribute more positive results? Take a few seconds to compare ten percent of nine hundred ninety with nine.

Pause: 4s

Reveal (spoken): Ten percent of nine hundred ninety is ninety-nine. The much larger group produces ninety-nine false positives, compared with nine true positives.

## Beat 2 — Choose the right group to count

Content needed: Preserve the identities and condition colors of the nine true positives and 99 false positives while regrouping them into one positive-results set. Remove negative results from the active view. Show 108 positive results in total, with nine blue marks and 99 grey marks at equal weight per person. Reveal 9 / (9 + 99) = 9 / 108 ≈ 8.3% in the order spoken. Contrast the reference sets for 9/10 and 9/108 without changing the nine true-positive identities.

Narration: Now suppose all we know about one person is that their result was positive. We should look only at the positive results. Bring the nine true positives together with the ninety-nine false positives. There are one hundred eight positive results altogether.

Narration: Of those one hundred eight, only nine belong to people with the condition. Nine divided by one hundred eight is about eight point three percent: roughly one in twelve.

Narration: Why isn't the answer ninety percent? Sensitivity starts with people who have the condition and asks how many test positive. Our question starts with people who test positive and asks how many have the condition. The nine people are the same, but the group we divide by changes from ten to one hundred eight.

## Beat 3 — Same test, different population

Content needed: Keep the first result, 9/108 ≈ 8.3%, as a compact reference. Introduce a different hypothetical population of 1,000 with 100 people with the condition and 900 without it: prevalence rises from 1% to 10%. Keep sensitivity at 90% and false-positive rate at 10%. Derive 90 true positives, 90 false positives, 180 total positives, and 90/180 = 50%. Do not depict specific people acquiring the condition. Preserve condition colors and positive-result marking. Present comparison as conditional on fixed test rates and the stated population model.

Narration: The starting proportion with the condition is called prevalence. Let's change it from one percent to ten percent in a different population of one thousand, while keeping both test rates fixed.

Narration: Now one hundred people have the condition. Ninety percent of them gives ninety true positives. Nine hundred people do not have it. Ten percent of them gives ninety false positives.

Narration: Among the one hundred eighty positive results, ninety have the condition: fifty percent, instead of eight point three percent. The test's sensitivity and false-positive rate haven't improved. The starting population changed.

Narration: Higher prevalence supplies more true positives and fewer false positives when those rates stay fixed. Lower prevalence does the reverse. To interpret a positive result in this model, count positives from both groups, then ask what fraction came from people with the condition.
