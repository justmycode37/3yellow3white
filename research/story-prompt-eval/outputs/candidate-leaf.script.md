# A leaf trades water for carbon

## Beat 1 — Carbon needs a way in

Content needed: An idealized leaf and enlarged lower-surface cutaway. Introduce the same guard-cell pair and pore used in all three beats; connected parts remain connected as the gap narrows in beat-3. GOLD dots mean carbon dioxide. Show movement both ways, with net inward movement as leaf cells use carbon dioxide. No water-vapor reveal yet.

Narration: To make sugar using sunlight, a leaf needs carbon dioxide from the air. But much of that work happens inside the leaf. How does the gas get there? Look at this tiny opening in the leaf’s surface. It is called a stoma. The gap leads into air spaces among the leaf’s cells. The gold dots represent carbon dioxide. Molecules move through the opening in both directions. As cells use carbon dioxide for photosynthesis, more can enter than leave. An open pore helps keep that carbon supply coming.

## Beat 2 — The same gap lets water out

Content needed: Keep the same open pore. BLUE dots mean water vapor from moist internal cell surfaces. Establish evaporation, then movement through the pore. Drier outside air supports net outward vapor movement while GOLD carbon dioxide has net inward movement. Paths are illustrative, not measurements or a one-for-one swap.

Narration: Now follow the blue dots: water. The surfaces of cells inside the leaf are moist. Some of that water evaporates into the air spaces as water vapor. The same opening that admits carbon dioxide also gives this vapor a route out. When the outside air is drier, more water vapor moves outward than inward. The opening does not sort the gases or exchange one water molecule for one carbon dioxide molecule. Both move through it. So gaining access to carbon dioxide also exposes the leaf to water loss.

## Beat 3 — A smaller gap has two effects

Content needed: Start with the same open pore and both gases. Guard-cell shape changes narrow the gap; show reduced passage for both species under the same outside conditions. Explain the response without conscious intent. Retain some water loss through other routes; closing a pore must not appear to seal the whole leaf.

Narration: In dry conditions, water loss can become difficult to replace. Signals within the plant change the water content and shape of the guard cells around the pore, narrowing the opening. The plant does not think through a choice; this is a biological response. With a smaller gap, less water vapor escapes through that route. But less carbon dioxide can enter, too, which can limit photosynthesis. Closing helps conserve water; it does not instantly stop all water loss. Some water can still escape through other routes. That is the tradeoff: the opening supports sugar-making while also letting precious water leave.
