# A leaf trades water for carbon

## Beat 1 — An opening with two consequences

Content needed: A schematic leaf with one selected stoma on its lower surface, enlarged into a connected cutaway. Show paired guard cells around an open pore, moist internal cell surfaces, and connected internal air spaces. Establish outside air containing carbon dioxide and internal air containing abundant water vapor. Reveal net carbon dioxide entry and net water vapor exit only with the corresponding narration. Molecules move in both directions; emphasize the net flows without treating the opening as a valve.

Narration: A leaf needs carbon dioxide from the air to make sugars through photosynthesis. But its outer covering makes it difficult for gases to pass straight through. Tiny openings called stomata provide a route inside. Here is one, enlarged, with two guard cells surrounding the opening. Beyond it are air spaces beside the leaf's cells. While photosynthesis uses carbon dioxide inside, more can enter from outside. That same open route also lets water vapor escape. So the opening helps the leaf get an ingredient for making sugar, but it also creates a route for losing water.

## Beat 2 — Why water keeps leaving

Content needed: Retain the same open pore and internal air spaces. Show a small amount of water evaporating from moist cell surfaces into internal air, followed by net vapor diffusion through the pore. Show carbon dioxide being taken up at photosynthesizing cells, maintaining net entry. Compare the same pore under less dry and drier outside air, holding its opening and temperature constant. Do not use equations or numerical readouts.

Narration: Why does water keep leaving? Inside the leaf, water evaporates from moist cell surfaces into the air spaces. When the outside air is drier, water vapor spreads outward overall. Individual molecules move both ways through the opening; it is not a one-way valve. Carbon dioxide also moves both ways, but photosynthesis removes it inside, helping maintain an overall inward flow. Now make the outside air drier while keeping this opening the same size. The greater difference in water vapor makes water loss faster. The leaf can keep gaining carbon, but its water supply may struggle to replace what escapes.

## Beat 3 — Narrow the route

Content needed: Carry the dry-air condition and the same pore into a partial, then near-complete closure driven by coordinated guard-cell shape change. Reduce both net transport streams as the passage narrows. Show a small continuing water-loss route through the leaf covering, without implying a fixed rate or perfect seal. Return to the same leaf with the selected region identified. Reveal the final tradeoff only after both transport consequences are visible.

Narration: In dry conditions, signals in the plant can cause guard cells to lose water and change shape, narrowing the opening. This response does not require the plant to think or choose. A narrower route slows water vapor escaping, helping protect the leaf's water supply. But it also restricts carbon dioxide entering. With less carbon dioxide available, photosynthesis can slow, so the leaf may make less sugar. Closing does not instantly stop every bit of water loss: some can still escape through the covering and any remaining openings. That is the tradeoff: an open route admits carbon more easily and loses water more easily; closing it conserves water while limiting carbon uptake.
