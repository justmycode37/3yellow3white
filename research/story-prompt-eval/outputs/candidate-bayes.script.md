# Why a positive screen is not a diagnosis

## Beat 1 — Two routes to a positive

Content needed: A hypothetical population of 1,000, represented by two bars on a common count scale: 10 with the condition in BLUE, 990 without it in GREY. State membership before showing results. Within those same bars, outline positive portions in YELLOW: 9 of 10 and 99 of 990. Keep membership colors visible. Label negative remainders 1 and 891 only if needed for the partition; do not display the final fraction yet. This is an exact toy population, not medical evidence.

Narration: Imagine a thousand people taking a screening test. Ten have a condition, and nine hundred ninety do not. This is a toy probability example, not medical guidance.

Narration: The test is positive for nine of the ten people with the condition. It catches ninety percent of that group. But it also gives a positive result to ninety-nine people without the condition: ten percent of that much larger group.

Narration: Those are two routes to the same positive result. A positive can come from someone with the condition, or from someone without it. To interpret a positive, we need to compare both routes.

## Beat 2 — Change the group we are counting

Content needed: Carry the same population bars. Extract their positive portions, retaining BLUE or GREY membership and YELLOW result outlines. Build one count-scaled positive bar from the two pieces: 9 with the condition, 99 without. The extraction filters by result; it does not change anyone's condition. Until Reveal, show those counts but hide 108, the quotient, percentages, and answer highlights. During Pause, freeze the evidence without new cues. At Reveal show 9 + 99 = 108, then 9/108 ≈ 8.3%.

Narration: Suppose all we know about a person is that their result is positive. We now restrict our attention to the positive group. The negative results leave our count, while the nine and the ninety-nine remain.

Invitation (spoken): Among these positives, roughly what fraction have the condition? Take five seconds to compare the two counts.

Pause: 5s

Reveal (spoken): There are a hundred eight positives altogether. Nine have the condition. Nine divided by a hundred eight is one in twelve, or about eight point three percent.

Narration: The ninety percent we started with answered a different question: among people with the condition, how many test positive? We are asking: among people testing positive, how many have the condition? The starting group changes, so the denominator changes.

## Beat 3 — Keep the test, change the population

Content needed: Retain the positive bar and its 9/108 result as a reference. Introduce a clearly distinct comparison population of 1,000 with 100 having the condition and 900 without. Use the same count scale and membership colors. Fix sensitivity at 90% and false-positive rate at 10%. Derive 90 true positives and 90 false positives, then their combined bar and 90/180 = 50%. The original people do not change membership; these are two hypothetical populations. Conclude with both positive bars compared on the common scale.

Narration: Now consider a different population of a thousand, with a hundred people who have the condition. Prevalence, the share who have it, has risen from one percent to ten percent.

Narration: Keep the test's two rates fixed. It still catches ninety percent of those with the condition, called its sensitivity. That now gives ninety positives. Its false-positive rate stays at ten percent. Among the nine hundred without the condition, that gives another ninety positives.

Narration: Now half the positives have the condition: ninety out of a hundred eighty. The test's rates stayed the same, but the balance between the two routes changed.

Narration: Higher prevalence supplies more true positives and fewer false positives in a population of fixed size. Lower prevalence does the reverse. A positive result becomes informative only when we combine how the test behaves with how common the condition was in the population being tested.
