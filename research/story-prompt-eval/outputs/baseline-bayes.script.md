# Why a positive screen is not a diagnosis

## Beat 1 — Two ways to test positive

Content needed: A hypothetical population of 1,000, partitioned into 10 with the condition and 990 without it. Of these groups, respectively 9 and 99 test positive. Preserve condition membership when marking test results. Show 9/10 = 90% and 99/990 = 10% only alongside their spoken explanations. Do not yet show the combined positive total or the answer.

Narration: A test detects ninety percent of people who have a condition. If your result is positive, does that mean you have a ninety percent chance?

Narration: Let's count people in a toy example, not a medical recommendation. Among a thousand people, ten have the condition and nine hundred ninety do not. The test is positive for nine of those ten. That ninety percent detection rate is called sensitivity.

Narration: But it also gives a positive result to ninety-nine people without the condition. Ninety-nine out of nine hundred ninety is ten percent: the false-positive rate. So a positive result can come from either group. The test catches most people with the condition, but the group without it is much larger.

## Beat 2 — Change the group we count

Content needed: Retain the same people and result marks. Gather the 9 positives with the condition and 99 positives without it into one positive-only group. Reveal its total of 108 as narrated. During the thinking pause retain the two counts and total, but withhold the fraction and probability. At Reveal show 9/(9+99) = 9/108 = 1/12, approximately 8.3%.

Narration: Our question starts with a positive result, so we must now look only at people who tested positive. Bring together the nine who have the condition and the ninety-nine who do not. That makes a hundred eight positive results.

Invitation (spoken): Of those hundred eight people, what fraction actually have the condition? Take a moment to choose the numerator and denominator.

Pause: 6s

Reveal (spoken): Nine out of a hundred eight: one in twelve, or about eight point three percent. We divide by all positive results because that is the group we know we belong to. Ninety percent answered a different question: among people with the condition, how many test positive? Reversing the question changes which group goes underneath the fraction.

## Beat 3 — Same test, different population

Content needed: Keep the first result as a compact comparison, then explicitly introduce a different hypothetical population of 1,000 with 100 having the condition and 900 without it. Keep sensitivity 90% and false-positive rate 10% fixed. Show 90 true positives and 90 false positives, then 90/(90+90) = 50%. Compare prevalence 1% versus 10%, and resulting probabilities approximately 8.3% versus 50%.

Narration: Now imagine a different thousand people: a hundred have the condition, and nine hundred do not. The condition's prevalence, its share of the population, has risen from one percent to ten percent. Keep the test's two rates unchanged.

Narration: Ninety percent of a hundred gives ninety positive results from people with the condition. Ten percent of nine hundred gives ninety from people without it. Now half of the hundred eighty positive results come from people with the condition.

Narration: The test has not improved. The mixture entering it has changed. Higher prevalence supplies more true positives and fewer false positives; lower prevalence does the reverse, with these rates fixed. To interpret a positive result in this model, count both routes to positive, then ask what share came from the condition. A positive result alone does not tell you that share.
