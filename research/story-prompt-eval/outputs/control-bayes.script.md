# Why a positive screen is not a diagnosis

## Beat 1 — Follow a thousand people

Content needed: Hypothetical cohort of 1,000: 10 with the condition, 990 without. The test is positive for 9 of the 10 and 99 of the 990. Show all four outcomes, including 1 negative with the condition and 891 negatives without it. Use count-proportional areas, with enlarged detail explicitly marked. Preserve condition status when adding positive-result outlines. Do not show the combined positive count or posterior fraction yet.

Narration: Imagine a screening test comes back positive. How much does that tell us? We need more than a description of how well the test works. We also need to know how common the condition was before testing.

Narration: Here is a made-up population of one thousand people. Ten have the condition, and nine hundred ninety do not. This is a toy probability example, not medical guidance.

Narration: Among the ten with the condition, nine test positive and one tests negative. Among the nine hundred ninety without it, ninety-nine test positive and eight hundred ninety-one test negative.

Narration: The test catches nine out of ten people who have the condition. That sounds reassuring. But a positive result can come from either group. We need to follow both routes.

## Beat 2 — Change the group you count

Content needed: Keep the four outcome groups from beat-1. Isolate the 9 true positives and 99 false positives while keeping status colors. Show 108 total positives only when narration combines them. During the thinking pause, show the two counts and total but withhold the fraction's value and percentage. At the reveal, show 9/108 = 1/12 ≈ 8.3%. Distinguish P(positive | condition) from P(condition | positive) using plain-language set labels before any notation.

Narration: Now suppose all we know about one person is that their test was positive. Which group should we count? Only people with positive results. The negative results no longer belong in our comparison.

Narration: Nine people with the condition tested positive. So did ninety-nine people without it. Together, that makes one hundred eight positive results.

Invitation (spoken): Of these one hundred eight people, only nine have the condition. About what fraction is that? Take a few seconds to think.

Pause: 5s

Reveal (spoken): Nine out of one hundred eight is one in twelve, or about eight point three percent.

Narration: The ninety percent we started with answers a different question: among people with the condition, how many test positive? We just answered: among people who test positive, how many have the condition?

Narration: The test result raises the chance from one percent to about eight percent. It provides evidence, but in this population the condition is still unlikely.

## Beat 3 — Keep the test, change the population

Content needed: Preserve the positive-result comparison. Introduce a second hypothetical population of 1,000 with 100 having the condition and 900 without it. Hold sensitivity at 90% and false-positive rate at 10%. Derive 90 true positives and 90 false positives, then 90/180 = 50%. Compare with the original 9 versus 99 and 8.3% result. Counts or area lengths must share a stated linear scale within each comparison; do not suggest the same individuals acquire the condition. Finish with the two positive groups and the rate invariants, without a clinical recommendation.

Narration: What if the condition is more common? Keep the test exactly the same: ninety percent of people with the condition test positive, and ten percent of people without it test positive.

Narration: Consider a different population of one thousand, with one hundred who have the condition and nine hundred who do not. Now ninety people with the condition test positive. Ten percent of the other nine hundred gives ninety positive results without the condition.

Narration: That is ninety people with the condition among one hundred eighty positive results: fifty percent. The test's two rates stayed fixed, but the meaning of a positive changed.

Narration: Why? A more common condition supplies more true positives, while the smaller group without it supplies fewer false positives. When the condition is rare, even a modest false-positive rate can produce many more false positives than true ones.

Narration: To interpret a positive result, count both ways it could occur, then ask what share came from people with the condition. The starting prevalence is part of that answer.
